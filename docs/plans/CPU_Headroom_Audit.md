# CPU headroom audit

The 32 Phase III development graphs supply the nominations. The 24 primary and four transfer graphs have already been examined in earlier work. They may be used to audit historical deployment costs, never to nominate a winner in this audit. The two counts on every call define a controlled compiler workload.

## Opportunity and replication

1. Use the existing development-selected joint cap-16 rule. Choose the largest allowed tile no greater than 16, using direct joint reduction when it covers the batch. Exclude the unobserved program and candidates with failed correctness checks.
2. For each graph, freeze the observed candidate with the lowest median of the three development process medians. Use the registered candidate order to break ties. Its difference from the fixed rule is the empirical headroom within this menu. Report the signed paired difference separately. Choosing a minimum on these same measurements is optimistic.
3. Test the fixed rule and the frozen nominee on **all 32** development graphs in five new, separately launched CPU processes. Equal choices run once. On the four convolution graphs with batches of four and eight also test joint one-image tiling, a simpler exception suggested by development. Time seed 42 after six warm-ups for 30 randomized rounds. Validate seeds 42, 1729 and 2026 against the ordinary FP32 output and the independent exact-count reference. Record compiler fingerprints, build time and the individual samples. This checks repeatability on known graphs.
4. Compute each process's paired saving and ratio. The repeat estimate is the median across the five processes. For each compared executable compute the 95th percentile over the six absolute differences between its four same-executable alias medians per process, pooled across processes. Use the larger of the two floors. A resolved gain or loss must exceed that floor and have a strict majority of process signs. This local repeatability diagnostic does not control a familywise false-positive rate.
5. Report separate development and repeat summaries, family totals, and the concentration of the absolute savings. Include zeros, losses, ties and cases that fail to replicate. Only the development menu has an exhaustive empirical optimum. Repeating a nominated pair does not identify a new optimum.

## Setup and stopping decisions

Use microseconds throughout. For horizon H, the incremental saving of candidate a over fixed implementation f is H times their latency difference, less the difference in their measured compile costs and any additional information cost. The most favorable finite-menu bound maximizes this quantity over all valid candidates, allowing different schedules to win at different horizons. The original benchmark's regime charges any baseline compilation required by the selector. It is also useful to show a zero-information-cost ceiling, reused calibration, and cold calibration. The baseline and fixed candidate are each charged once when required. Data collection, offline training and common initialization are outside the deployment accounting. The screening bound gives the selector the winning identity for free. Actual static and timed choices use their recorded setup costs and the same cap-16 comparator.

Report horizons 1, 10, 100 and 1,000, with 10,000 as a long-reuse sensitivity. Cases that only win because their compilation is cheaper are distinguished from genuine positive per-call savings.

The opportunity decision concerns the measured CPU and this menu. If none of the nominations has a repeatable advantage, retain the fixed policy and close the search. If gains concentrate, test a cheap exception before fitting a general selector. Specifically, advance the one-image convolution exception to a bounded test on unseen shapes only if it beats cap-16 by more than the local measurement floor and by at least 5% on all four old batch-four/eight convolution graphs in the new processes. This cut-off is a follow-up design choice made after seeing development data and before the repeat. It has no bearing on the original Phase III endpoint.

An information-advantage claim requires a new graph, decisions frozen before testing, a direct comparison with source-only information, changed decisions that improve the measured result, and accounting for the cost of collecting candidate IR. A useful scoped source rule can stand on its own. GPU transfer is a separate question.
